This week 2 Kings ends with Jerusalem's fall. In chapter 21, Hezekiah's son Manasseh undoes his father's work: he rebuilds the high places, erects altars for Baal, builds an altar to the host of heaven in both temple courtyards, puts an Asherah image in the temple itself, makes his son pass through the fire, and fills Jerusalem with innocent blood. Through the prophets God announces that he will wipe Jerusalem as one wipes a dish and turns it upside down. Manasseh's son Amon follows his ways and is killed by his servants, and the people make his eight-year-old son Josiah king.

In chapter 22, Josiah does right and does not turn aside to the right or the left. During temple repairs, the high priest Hilkiah finds the scroll of the law. When it is read to the king, he tears his clothes and sends to the prophetess Huldah. She answers that the disaster written in the scroll will come, because the people have abandoned God, but because Josiah humbled himself and wept, he will go to his grave in peace and not see it. In chapter 23, Josiah reads the scroll to all the people and makes a covenant before God, and they join it. He clears out everything made for Baal, Asherah and the host of heaven, defiles Topheth so no one can pass a child through the fire for Molech, and defiles the high places Solomon built for foreign gods. At Bethel he tears down Jeroboam's altar and burns bones on it, as a man of God from Judah had proclaimed long before (1 Kings 13:2 names Josiah), and he keeps a Passover like none since the days of the judges. No king turned to God like him with all his heart, soul and might, yet God does not turn from his anger over Manasseh. Josiah is killed at Megiddo by Pharaoh Neco, who deposes his son Jehoahaz and makes another son king as Jehoiakim.

In chapter 24, Jehoiakim serves Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and then rebels, and God sends raiders against Judah, as the text says, for Manasseh's sins and the innocent blood he shed. His son Jehoiachin surrenders after three months, and Nebuchadnezzar takes the temple and palace treasures and deports the king, the leaders, the warriors and craftsmen, leaving only the poorest. Zedekiah, made king in his place, also rebels. In chapter 25, Babylon besieges Jerusalem until famine sets in. Zedekiah flees but is caught; his sons are killed before his eyes, and he is blinded and taken to Babylon. The temple, the palace and the houses of Jerusalem are burned, the walls torn down, and more people deported. Gedaliah, appointed over those left, is murdered, and the people flee to Egypt. The book closes with Jehoiachin, in the thirty-seventh year of his exile, released from prison and eating in the king of Babylon's presence for the rest of his life.

1 Chronicles opens by tracing the family line from Adam through Noah's sons to Abraham, then through Ishmael, Isaac, and Esau and the kings and chiefs of Edom. Psalm 82 pictures God taking his stand in the divine assembly and calling to account those who judge unjustly instead of rescuing the poor and needy, and asks God to rise and judge the earth. Psalm 83 asks God not to stay silent while nations, Assyria among them, plot to wipe out Israel's name, so that they may know he alone is Most High over all the earth. After Jerusalem's fall, these prayers for God to judge the nations carry fresh weight, though that connection is an interpretation.
