Job opens in Uz with a man the text calls blameless and upright, who fears God and turns from evil; he is also very rich. In chapter 1 the scene moves to heaven, where the sons of God come before the LORD and Satan comes with them. God points to Job; Satan asks whether Job fears God for nothing and says that if everything is taken away Job will curse God to his face. God allows it, but not against Job himself. In one day messengers report that the Sabeans took the oxen and donkeys, fire from heaven burned up the flocks, the Chaldeans carried off the camels, and a great wind collapsed the house where his children were feasting, killing them. Job tears his robe, shaves his head and worships: he came naked from the womb and will return naked; the LORD gives and the LORD takes; blessed be his name. The narrator adds that he did not sin.

In chapter 2 the heavenly scene repeats, and Satan is allowed to strike Job's body but spare his life. Covered in sores from head to foot, Job sits in the ashes scraping himself with a piece of pottery. His wife tells him to curse God and die; he asks whether they should receive good from God and not evil. Three friends, Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite, come to comfort him, weep when they see him, and sit with him seven days and nights without a word. Then, in chapter 3, Job curses the day he was born. He wishes he had died at birth and were at rest where the weary rest and the slave is free, and asks why light is given to those who long for death.

Eliphaz answers in chapters 4 and 5. He asks who ever perished innocent, since in his experience those who plow trouble reap it, and tells of a spirit that came to him at night asking whether a mortal can be righteous before God. He urges Job to seek God, calls the one God reproves happy, and promises safety and a ripe old age. He insists this has been searched out and is true, but in 42:7 God tells Eliphaz that he and his two friends have not spoken what is right, so his reasoning is one voice in the debate, not the book's verdict. In chapter 6 Job replies that his grief would outweigh the sand of the seas, that the arrows of Shaddai are in him, and that he wishes God would crush him. He calls his friends as unreliable as streams that dry up in the heat and asks them to show him where he has gone wrong. In chapter 7 he turns to God: his days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle, his life is a breath. He asks why God watches him so closely and, if he has sinned, why God does not pardon him.

Psalm 109, a psalm of David, is the prayer of someone repaid with hatred for love. A long middle section calls down curses on an enemy: few days, another to take his office, orphaned children. The psalmist then asks God to act for his name's sake, since he is poor and needy, and ends praising the God who stands at the right hand of the needy. One way to read the pairing is that, like Job, the psalm is unguarded speech to God from someone who feels wrongly treated.
