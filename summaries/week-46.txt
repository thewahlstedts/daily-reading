Ecclesiastes is the words of the Teacher, son of David and king in Jerusalem. Chapter 1 opens with his refrain, vanity of vanities, and the question of what a person gains from all his toil under the sun. The sun, the wind and the streams go around and around, and there is nothing new under the sun. He set out to search by wisdom all that is done, and found it all vanity and chasing wind, for much wisdom brings much frustration. In chapter 2 he tests pleasure, wine, building, gardens, wealth and singers, denying himself nothing, and still finds no profit. Wisdom is better than folly as light is better than darkness, yet the wise man and the fool die alike and are both forgotten, and what he has worked for will pass to someone who may be a fool. So he hated life. Yet the chapter ends on a note he will return to: there is nothing better than to eat and drink and find delight in one's work, and this too is from the hand of God.

Chapter 3 is the well-known poem of times: a time to bear and a time to die, to weep and to laugh, to be silent and to speak, for war and for peace. God has made everything suitable in its time and put a sense of the past in human hearts, yet no one can grasp what God does from beginning to end. He says God will judge the righteous and the wicked, yet notes that humans and beasts alike return to dust. Chapter 4 looks at the tears of the oppressed with no one to comfort them, and at the lone man toiling with no one to toil for. Two are better than one, because if one falls the other can help him up, and a threefold cord is not easily broken.

Chapter 5 turns to worship: guard your steps in the house of God, draw near to listen, let your words be few because God is in heaven and you are on earth, and fulfill what you vow. Whoever loves money is never satisfied with it, and as everyone comes from the womb naked, so he departs, taking nothing for his toil. Again the Teacher calls it good to eat, drink and enjoy one's work in the days God gives, and calls the power to enjoy wealth a gift of God. Chapter 6 describes the opposite misfortune, a man given wealth and honor whom God does not enable to enjoy it, and ends by asking who knows what is good for a person in his few fleeting days, or what the future will bring.

Psalms 122–124 are songs of ascents. Psalm 122 rejoices at going up to the house of the LORD and prays for the peace of Jerusalem. In Psalm 123 the people look to God as servants look to their master's hand, asking for grace after having their fill of contempt. Psalm 124 says that if the LORD had not been on Israel's side, its enemies would have swallowed it alive and the waters swept over it; instead it escaped like a bird from a broken snare, and its help is in the name of the LORD, maker of heaven and earth. Set beside Ecclesiastes, one way to read them is as a steady counterweight: where the Teacher finds little to hold onto under the sun, these songs look up to the maker of heaven and earth.
