Isaiah's vision concerns Judah and Jerusalem in the days of four kings, from Uzziah to Hezekiah. Chapter 1 calls heaven and earth to listen: God raised children who rebelled; even an ox knows its owner, but Israel does not. God is weary of their sacrifices and festivals, because their hands are full of blood; instead they must wash, stop doing evil, seek justice and defend the orphan and the widow. Come, let us argue it out, God says: though your sins are like scarlet, they will be white as snow, but if you refuse and rebel, the sword will devour you. The faithful city has become a whore, yet God will purify it like metal and it will again be called the city of righteousness.

Chapter 2 looks to days to come: the mountain of the LORD's house raised above the hills, all nations streaming to it to learn his ways, and swords beaten into plowshares, with nation no longer learning war. Then it turns to the present: the land is full of silver, horses and idols, and on the LORD's day all human pride will be brought low. Chapters 3–4 describe God taking away Jerusalem's support, leaving children to rule and people oppressing each other, and confronting the leaders who grind the faces of the poor. But in that day the branch of the LORD will be beautiful, those left in Zion will be called holy, and God will spread over Mount Zion a cloud by day and fire by night as a shelter. Chapter 5 is the song of the vineyard: the owner did everything for it, and it produced only wild grapes. The vineyard is the house of Israel; God looked for justice and found bloodshed. Woes follow on those who add house to house, chase strong drink, and call evil good and good evil.

Chapter 6 is Isaiah's call, in the year King Uzziah died. He sees the Lord on a high throne, the hem of his robe filling the temple, and seraphs calling, Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory. Isaiah cries that he is ruined, a man of unclean lips, and a seraph touches his mouth with a coal from the altar to take away his guilt. When the Lord asks whom he shall send, Isaiah offers himself and is given a hard message for a people who will hear and not understand, until the land lies waste and only a stump is left, though the holy seed will be that stump.

Chapter 7 is set when Rezin of Aram and Pekah of Israel march against Jerusalem and King Ahaz's heart shakes like trees in the wind. Isaiah tells him not to fear these two smoldering stumps; their plan will not stand, but without faith they will not endure. Ahaz refuses to ask for a sign, and Isaiah says the Lord will give one anyway: the virgin is with child, she will bear a son and call him God with us, and before the boy knows to choose good over evil, the land of the two kings Ahaz fears will be abandoned. Then the king of Assyria will come. In its setting it is a sign to the house of David in Ahaz's day; Matthew 1:23 quotes it about the birth of Jesus, and Christians have read it in that light. Psalms 131–132 close the week: a soul quieted like a weaned child, and a prayer recalling David's vow to find a place for the LORD and God's oath to set one of David's descendants on his throne, with Zion as his resting place forever.
