In Nehemiah 5 poor families cry out that to buy grain in a famine and pay the king's tax they have mortgaged their fields and houses and are even handing their sons and daughters over as slaves, to their own fellow Jews. Nehemiah is angry. He confronts the nobles and officials for charging interest to their brothers, and they agree to give back the fields, vineyards and houses and stop taking interest, under oath before the priests. He adds that in twelve years as governor he never took the governor's food allowance, unlike earlier governors, though he fed a hundred and fifty people at his table, and he asks God to remember him for good.

In chapter 6 Sanballat and Geshem invite Nehemiah four times to a meeting in the plain of Ono, meaning to harm him, and four times he answers that he is doing a great work and cannot come down. A fifth message, an open letter, accuses him of planning a revolt and of hiring prophets to proclaim him king. Then Shemaiah urges him to hide inside the temple because men are coming to kill him, but Nehemiah realizes Tobiah and Sanballat have hired him to frighten him into sin, and refuses. The wall is finished in fifty-two days, and the surrounding nations lose heart, knowing God did it. In chapter 7 Nehemiah sets guards and notes that the city is large but has few people, then finds the record of those who first returned, a list much like Ezra 2.

In chapter 8, in the seventh month, the people gather at the Water Gate and ask Ezra to bring the law of Moses. He reads it from dawn until noon from a wooden platform, and the Levites explain it so they understand. The people weep at what they hear, but Nehemiah, Ezra and the Levites tell them the day is holy, that they should eat, drink and share with those who have nothing, because the joy of the LORD is their refuge. Finding in the law the command to live in booths, they keep that festival with great joy, as Israel had not done since the days of Jeshua (Joshua) son of Nun. In chapter 9 they gather again, this time fasting in sackcloth, and the Levites lead a long prayer that retells Israel's story: God chose Abram, freed them from Egypt, gave the law at Sinai and fed them in the desert. Again and again the people rebelled, and again and again God, gracious and compassionate, did not abandon them. The prayer ends with the people still slaves in their own land, its harvest going to foreign kings, and they make a written agreement. Chapter 10 lists those who seal it, led by Nehemiah, and spells out the promises: no marrying outside the people, no buying and selling on the Sabbath, debts cancelled in the seventh year, and regular giving so they will not neglect the house of their God.

Psalm 105 tells much of the same story as a song of thanks: God remembered his covenant with Abraham, sent Joseph ahead into Egypt, sent Moses and Aaron with signs, and brought his people out with joy. The plan sets it beside the prayer of Nehemiah 9, which invites hearing the two side by side, though that pairing is an interpretation.
