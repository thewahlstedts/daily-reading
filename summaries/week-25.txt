Luke 13 opens with news of Galileans killed by Pilate, and Jesus adds the eighteen killed when the tower in Siloam fell. He says they were no worse sinners than anyone else, but unless his hearers repent they will all perish too, and he tells of a fruitless fig tree given one more year. He heals a woman bent over for eighteen years on the Sabbath and answers the indignant synagogue ruler that she, a daughter of Abraham, should be set free on that day. He compares the kingdom of God to a mustard seed and to yeast, urges people to enter through the narrow door, and says people will come from east and west to recline at table in the kingdom. Warned that Herod wants to kill him, he keeps on toward Jerusalem and laments over the city that kills its prophets, which he longed to gather as a hen gathers her brood under her wings.

In chapter 14, at a Pharisee's house on the Sabbath, Jesus heals a man with edema and tells guests not to grab the places of honor, since whoever exalts himself will be humbled. In a parable, invited guests make excuses, so the master fills his banquet with the poor and the people from the roads. Then he tells the crowds to count the cost of following him, as a builder or a king would. Chapter 15 answers the complaint that he welcomes sinners and eats with them, with three stories of the lost: a sheep, a coin, and a younger son who wastes his inheritance and comes home to a father who runs to meet him and throws a feast. The older brother refuses to go in, and the father pleads with him that his brother was dead and is alive, was lost and is found.

Chapter 16 tells of a shrewd manager who cuts what his master's debtors owe to make friends, and Jesus says no one can serve God and money. Then comes a rich man who feasts every day while poor Lazarus lies at his gate; after death the rich man is in torment and begs Abraham to send Lazarus to warn his five brothers, but Abraham says they have Moses and the prophets, and if they will not listen to them, they will not be convinced even if someone rises from the dead. In chapter 17 Jesus tells the disciples to forgive a brother who repents, even seven times a day. Of ten lepers he cleanses, only one, a Samaritan, comes back to thank him. He says the kingdom of God is in their midst and that the day of the Son of Man will be like the days of Noah and Lot, when life went on as usual until destruction came.

Psalms 98 and 99 call the whole earth to sing a new song to the LORD who is king, coming to judge the world with righteousness, the holy God who answered Moses, Aaron and Samuel, forgiving them yet punishing their wrongs. Psalm 100 calls all the earth to enter his gates with thanksgiving, because he made them, they are his sheep, and his loyal love is forever. Psalm 101, a psalm of David, vows to walk with integrity in his own house and keep the deceitful and arrogant away. Next to the parables of the lost, the call to come in with thanksgiving can read as an echo of the father's feast, though that pairing is an interpretation.
