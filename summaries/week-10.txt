This week belongs mostly to Elisha. In chapter 3, Joram son of Ahab, king of Israel, removes Baal's stone pillars but keeps to the sins of Jeroboam. When Mesha king of Moab rebels, Joram, Jehoshaphat of Judah and the king of Edom march against him and run out of water. Elisha says he would not look at Joram if not for Jehoshaphat, then tells them to dig cisterns in the wadi: without wind or rain it will fill. By morning it does, the Moabites take the water's red gleam for blood, rush in for plunder and are routed, until their king offers his firstborn son as a burnt offering on the wall and Israel withdraws. Chapter 4 is a run of smaller wonders: a prophet's widow fills every container she can borrow from one jar of oil and saves her children from her creditor; a woman of Shunem who gave Elisha a room is promised a son, and when the boy later dies on her lap, Elisha prays and stretches himself on him until he sneezes seven times and opens his eyes; flour makes poisoned stew safe; twenty barley loaves feed a hundred men.

In chapter 5, Naaman, commander of Aram's army, has a skin disease, and a girl taken captive from Israel points him to the prophet in Samaria. Told to wash seven times in the Jordan, he is offended until his servants talk him round, and his flesh becomes like a small boy's. He declares that there is no God in all the world except in Israel; Elisha refuses his gift, but Gehazi runs after him with a lie, takes silver and clothing, and leaves with Naaman's disease. In chapter 6 a borrowed ax head floats, and Elisha warns Israel's king of Aram's moves. When Aram surrounds him at Dothan, his frightened servant's eyes are opened to a mountain full of horses and chariots of fire; Elisha leads the blinded Arameans into Samaria, and the king feeds them and sends them home. Then Ben-Hadad besieges Samaria, a mother tells the king she has eaten her son, and the king swears to have Elisha's head.

In chapter 7, Elisha promises cheap flour in the gate by the next day, and the king's officer scoffs that it could not happen even if God made windows in heaven. Four men with a skin disease find the Aramean camp empty, because God had made the Arameans hear the sound of a great army. They decide not to keep this day of good news to themselves, the city plunders the camp, prices fall as Elisha said, and the officer is trampled in the gate. In chapter 8, the Shunammite comes back from seven years among the Philistines just as Gehazi is telling the king about her son, and her land is restored. Elisha weeps before Hazael, foreseeing the harm he will do Israel; Hazael smothers the sick Ben-Hadad and becomes king of Aram. In Judah, Joram son of Jehoshaphat, married to Ahab's daughter, follows the house of Ahab, yet God spares Judah for David's sake.

Psalm 79, a psalm of Asaph, mourns a defiled temple, a ruined Jerusalem and unburied dead. It asks how long God will be angry, pleads with him to forgive for his name's sake, and asks why the nations should say, "Where is their God?" One way to read the pairing is as a prayer for a week of siege and famine, though the psalm itself speaks of Jerusalem, not Samaria.
