In chapter 20 Zophar the Naamathite speaks for the second time. He feels insulted by Job's rebuke and insists on what he says has been known since humans were placed on earth: the joy of the wicked lasts only a moment. Wickedness may taste sweet in his mouth, but it turns to viper's venom in his stomach; he swallows riches and God makes him vomit them up, because he oppressed the poor and seized houses he did not build. Chapter 21 is Job's direct answer to that whole line of argument. Look at the wicked, he says: they live long and grow powerful, their children are settled around them, their houses are safe, their little ones dance to lyre and flute, and they go down to Sheol in peace, though they tell God to leave them alone. How often, he asks, is the lamp of the wicked actually put out? One man dies at ease and another in bitterness, and both lie in the dust. The friends' comfort, he concludes, is empty.

Eliphaz opens the third round in chapter 22 and goes further than before. Asking whether Job's righteousness is of any use to God, he tells Job his wickedness is great and charges him with specific wrongs: taking pledges from his family for nothing, stripping the naked, refusing water to the weary and bread to the hungry, and sending widows away empty-handed. The narrator called Job blameless (1:1), and Job answers these very charges in chapters 29 and 31. Then he urges Job to make peace with God and let God be his gold, and promises that his prayers will be heard. In chapter 23 Job says he would find God if he could and lay his case before him; he believes God would listen and he would be acquitted. But he looks forward and back, left and right, and cannot find him. Still, he says, God knows the way he takes, and when he has been tested he will come out like gold, for he has kept God's way and treasured his words more than daily food. Yet he trembles, for God does whatever he wishes. In chapter 24 he asks why God does not set times for judgment, and describes those who move boundary stones, take the orphan's donkey and the widow's ox, and leave the poor to sleep naked in the cold, while the cry of the wounded goes up from the city and, he says, God does not treat it as wrong. The chapter ends with the wicked brought low, cut off like heads of grain, and Job's challenge to anyone to prove him wrong.

Bildad's third speech in chapter 25 is only six verses: dominion and dread belong to God, even the moon and stars are not pure in his sight, so how much less a human being, a maggot and a worm. Then Psalms 114 and 115 turn to praise. Psalm 114 recalls Israel going out of Egypt, when the sea fled, the Jordan turned back and the mountains skipped like rams, and calls the earth to tremble before the God of Jacob, who turned rock into a pool of water. Psalm 115 asks God to take the glory not for his people but for his own name; idols of silver and gold have mouths, eyes and ears but cannot speak, see or hear, and those who make them become like them. Israel, the house of Aaron and all who fear the LORD are called to trust him as their help and shield. One way to hear the pairing is that, after so many words about God, these psalms speak to him and of his deeds.
