Chapter 20 opens with Joab taking Rabbah of the Ammonites while David stays in Jerusalem. The Ammonite king's crown, a talent of gold set with a precious stone, is placed on David's head, and then David's men strike down three Philistine champions born to the giants, among them Lahmi the brother of Goliath. In chapter 21 Satan stands against Israel and urges David to count the people. Joab protests that this will bring guilt on Israel but is overruled. God strikes Israel, and David confesses his sin. Through Gad the seer, God offers three choices: three years of famine, three months of devastation by enemies, or three days of the sword of the LORD. David chooses to fall into God's hand, because his compassion is very great. Seventy thousand men die, and God stops the angel before he destroys Jerusalem. Seeing the angel's drawn sword over the city, David asks that the punishment fall on him and his father's house, since the people, these sheep, have done nothing. Ornan the Jebusite offers his threshing floor and everything for the offerings as a gift, but David insists on paying full price, refusing to offer burnt offerings that cost him nothing. He builds an altar, God answers with fire from heaven, and the angel puts his sword away.

In chapter 22 David declares that this spot will be the house of God and the altar for Israel. Because Solomon is young and inexperienced, David gathers materials ahead of time. He tells Solomon that God would not let him build, since he had shed much blood, but promised a son, a man of rest, who would build the house and whose throne God would establish forever. David charges him to keep God's law and to be strong and courageous, and tells Israel's leaders to help. Chapters 23–24 begin with the old king making Solomon king and then organizing the Levites. Since God has given his people rest and dwells in Jerusalem, the Levites no longer need to carry the tabernacle; they assist the priests and stand every morning and evening to thank and praise God. The priests, descended from Aaron's sons Eleazar and Ithamar, are divided by lot into twenty-four divisions.

Chapters 25–26 set apart the families of Asaph, Heman and Jeduthun to prophesy with stringed instruments, harps and cymbals and assign their turns by lot, small and great alike, teacher with student. Gatekeepers draw lots for the gates, and others guard treasuries full of things dedicated from battle spoils, some by Samuel, Saul, Abner and Joab. Chapter 27 lists twelve army divisions, each serving one month a year, the tribal leaders, the king's stewards and his advisers, and recalls that Joab's count was never finished.

Psalm 90, a prayer of Moses, closes the week. God is from everlasting to everlasting, while human life is like grass that blossoms in the morning and withers by evening, seventy or eighty years that pass quickly. The psalm asks God to teach his people to number their days, to satisfy them in the morning with his loyal love, and to establish the work of their hands. Read after a week of David preparing a house he will not live to see, the prayer can sound like a fitting comment, though that pairing is an interpretation rather than something the text states.
