In Nehemiah 11 the people cast lots to bring one in ten to live in Jerusalem, and they bless those who volunteer; the rest of the chapter lists who settled where. Chapter 12 lists the priests and Levites, then describes the dedication of the wall. The priests and Levites purify themselves, the people, the gates and the wall, and Nehemiah sets two great choirs on top of the wall. They process in opposite directions, Ezra leading one and Nehemiah following the other, and meet at the house of God. There are great sacrifices, the women and children rejoice too, and the joy of Jerusalem is heard far away.

Chapter 13 ends on a sober note. Nehemiah, back from a time away at the king's court, finds that Eliashib the priest has given Tobiah a large room in the temple courts; he throws out Tobiah's belongings and has the rooms cleansed. The Levites have not been paid and have gone back to their fields, so he rebukes the officials and restores the tithes. Goods are being brought and sold in Jerusalem on the Sabbath, so he shuts the city gates for the day and warns off the merchants camping outside the wall. And Jews have married women from Ashdod, Ammon and Moab, and half their children cannot speak the language of Judah. He makes them swear not to give or take daughters in marriage, recalling that foreign wives led even Solomon to sin, and drives away a grandson of Eliashib who had married into Sanballat's family. Three times in the chapter he asks God to remember him. One way to read the chapter is as the promises of chapter 10 (no foreign marriages, no Sabbath trade, not neglecting the house of God) coming undone, though that link is an interpretation the text does not spell out.

In Hebrews 1 the author says that God, who once spoke through the prophets in many ways, has now spoken by a Son, the heir of all things and the radiance of God's glory, who made purification for sins and sat down at God's right hand. A chain of Scripture quotations then shows the Son as greater than the angels, who are servants sent to help those who will inherit salvation. Chapter 2 draws the first warning: if the message given through angels was binding, there is no escape for those who neglect so great a salvation. The author explains that Jesus was made lower than the angels for a short time so that he might taste death for everyone, sharing flesh and blood with his brothers to free those enslaved by fear of death; because he suffered when tempted, he can help those who are tempted, as a merciful and faithful high priest. In chapter 3 Jesus is compared with Moses: Moses was faithful as a servant in God's house, Christ as a son over it. The author quotes the warning not to harden hearts "today" when God speaks, recalls that the wilderness generation could not enter God's rest because of unbelief, and urges readers to encourage one another daily.

Psalm 106 tells Israel's history as a confession: at the sea, in the wilderness, at Horeb with the calf, at Peor and in the land, the people forgot God and rebelled, and yet he heard their cry and remembered his covenant. It ends asking God to gather them from the nations.
