In chapter 22 Paul speaks to the crowd from the barracks steps in Aramaic: born in Tarsus, brought up in Jerusalem under Gamaliel, he once persecuted the Way, until a light from heaven stopped him near Damascus and Jesus the Nazarene spoke to him. Later, in the temple, the Lord told him he would be sent far away to the Gentiles, and at that word the crowd erupts. When the tribune orders him flogged, Paul reveals that he was born a Roman citizen.

In chapter 23 Paul stands before the Sanhedrin. Declaring himself a Pharisee on trial over the resurrection of the dead, he sets the Pharisees and Sadducees against each other, and the tribune has to rescue him. That night the Lord stands by him and says that as he has testified in Jerusalem, he must also testify in Rome. More than forty men swear not to eat or drink until they have killed him, but Paul's nephew hears of the ambush, and the tribune sends him under heavy guard to Governor Felix in Caesarea. In chapter 24 the lawyer Tertullus calls Paul a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes, and Paul answers that he worships the God of the fathers according to the Way, hoping for the resurrection. Felix puts the case off, grows afraid when Paul speaks to him and Drusilla about righteousness, self-control and the coming judgment, hopes Paul will give him money, and after two years leaves Paul in prison for his successor, Festus.

In chapter 25 the chief priests ask Festus to bring Paul to Jerusalem, planning an ambush on the way. When Festus asks whether he will be tried there, Paul appeals to Caesar. King Agrippa and Bernice arrive, and Festus tells them the dispute is about their religion and a certain Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul claimed to be alive. In chapter 26 Paul tells Agrippa his story again: the light brighter than the sun, the voice telling him it is hard to kick against the goads, and the commission to open the Gentiles' eyes so they may turn from darkness to light. Festus shouts that his learning has driven him mad, and Agrippa asks whether Paul thinks he can make him a Christian so quickly. Leaving, they agree he has done nothing deserving death, and Agrippa says he could have been released if he had not appealed to Caesar.

Chapter 27 is the voyage. Paul warns against sailing on from Fair Havens, but the centurion Julius listens to the shipmaster, and a northeaster drives the ship for days until all hope is lost. Paul tells them an angel has promised that no life will be lost and urges the 276 people on board to eat. The ship runs aground and breaks up, the centurion stops the soldiers from killing the prisoners, and everyone reaches land. In chapter 28, on Malta, a viper fastens on Paul's hand without harming him, and he heals the father of Publius and many others. In Rome he lives under guard, explains the kingdom of God to the Jewish leaders from Moses and the prophets, and when some refuse to believe, quotes Isaiah and says this salvation has been sent to the Gentiles. The book ends with Paul in his rented house for two years, welcoming all who come and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ without hindrance, with no word of how his appeal turned out.
