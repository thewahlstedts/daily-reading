Hebrews 4 picks up the word "today" from the quotation in chapter 3 (Psalm 95:7-11). The author argues that God's rest was not used up when Israel entered the land, since if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken later of another day; so a sabbath rest still remains for God's people, and readers should make every effort to enter it. God's word is living and sharper than a double-edged sword. Then the author turns to the high priest: Jesus has gone through the heavens, was tempted in all things in the same way yet without sin, and can sympathize with weakness, so the author urges readers to approach the throne of grace with confidence.

Chapter 5 explains what a high priest is. He deals gently with the ignorant because he is weak himself, and he does not take the honor for himself but is called by God, as Aaron was. In the same way, Christ was appointed by God, who called him a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek (quoting Psalm 110:4). In the days of his flesh he prayed with loud cries and tears, learned obedience through what he suffered, and became the source of eternal salvation. The author then pauses to scold the readers: they ought to be teachers by now but still need milk, not solid food. Chapter 6 urges them to move on to maturity and contains one of the letter's sharpest warnings, about those who have tasted the heavenly gift and shared in the Holy Spirit and then fall away; Christian traditions read this passage in different ways, and the text itself goes straight on to say the author is convinced of better things concerning them and that God will not forget their love. It ends with God's oath to Abraham as an anchor for hope.

Chapter 7 turns to Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of the Most High God, who blessed Abraham and received a tenth from him (Genesis 14:18-20). The author argues that since Abraham gave him a tenth, Melchizedek is greater than Levi's priesthood, and that God's promise of a new priest in Melchizedek's order, from the tribe of Judah, shows the old priesthood could not bring perfection. Jesus became priest by an oath, holds the priesthood permanently because he lives forever, and always lives to intercede; unlike other high priests, he offered a sacrifice once for all when he offered himself. Chapter 8 states the main point: this high priest sits at God's right hand and serves in the true tabernacle, of which the earthly one is a shadow. He is mediator of a better covenant, and the author quotes the promise of a new covenant written on hearts, in which God will no longer remember their sins (Jeremiah 31:31-34). In chapter 9, where the earthly high priest entered the inner room once a year with blood, Christ entered once for all into the true sanctuary, heaven itself, by his own blood, to cleanse consciences, and will appear a second time to bring salvation to those who wait for him.

Psalm 107 is a song for the redeemed: wanderers in the desert, prisoners in darkness, fools afflicted to the gates of death and sailors in a storm all cry out to God in their trouble, and he rescues them. Each time the refrain calls them to thank him for his loyal love.
