The northern kingdom comes to its end this week. Chapter 15 moves quickly. In Judah, Azariah (also called Uzziah) does right but leaves the high places, and God strikes him with a skin disease, so he lives apart while his son Jotham governs. In Israel, Jeroboam's son Zechariah reigns six months before Shallum kills him, and the text notes that this fulfills God's word that Jehu's line would hold the throne to the fourth generation. Shallum lasts a month before Menahem kills him; Menahem pays Pul king of Assyria a thousand talents of silver, taken from Israel's wealthy, to keep his hold on the kingdom. His son Pekahiah is killed by his officer Pekah, and in Pekah's days Tiglath-Pileser of Assyria takes Galilee, Gilead and all Naphtali and deports the people, before Hoshea kills Pekah. In chapter 16, Ahaz of Judah even makes his son pass through the fire. When Aram and Israel attack Jerusalem, he calls himself the servant and son of the king of Assyria, sends him silver and gold from the temple, and is rescued as Assyria captures Damascus. Then, after seeing an altar in Damascus, he has the priest Uriah build a copy, makes it the main altar, and moves God's bronze altar aside.

Chapter 17 tells the fall. Hoshea stops paying tribute and sends to Egypt, and the king of Assyria imprisons him, besieges Samaria for three years, captures it, and deports Israel to Assyria. The writer stops to explain why: Israel feared other gods, built high places, set up pillars and Asherah poles, made two calves of metal, served Baal, and passed their children through the fire, though God had warned Israel and Judah through every prophet to turn back. They did not listen and stiffened their necks like their ancestors, so God removed them from his presence, leaving only Judah, which also failed to keep his commands. The king of Assyria settles foreigners in Samaria's towns; when lions kill some of them, a deported priest is sent back to Bethel to teach them, but each nation goes on making its own gods, fearing God while serving their idols.

Chapters 18 to 20 bring a different kind of king. Hezekiah trusts God as none of Judah's kings before or after him, removes the high places, and breaks up the bronze serpent Moses made, because people had been burning incense to it. He rebels against Assyria, and Sennacherib takes Judah's fortified cities. Hezekiah pays him with the temple's silver and the gold stripped from its doors, yet Assyria's officers still come to Jerusalem, and one of them shouts to the people on the wall in their own language that no god of any nation has rescued its land from Assyria. In chapter 19, Hezekiah goes to the temple in sackcloth and sends to the prophet Isaiah, who answers that God will send the king home by a rumor. When a threatening letter comes, Hezekiah spreads it out before God and prays that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that he alone is God. Isaiah brings God's answer: Sennacherib's arrogance has reached him, he will turn him back the way he came, and he will defend the city for his own sake and David's. That night the angel of God strikes the Assyrian camp, and Sennacherib goes home to Nineveh, where his own sons kill him. In chapter 20, God hears Hezekiah's prayer and tears in sickness and adds fifteen years to his life, with the shadow going back ten steps as a sign. But Hezekiah shows envoys from Babylon all his treasures, and Isaiah says that everything in the palace and some of his own descendants will one day be carried to Babylon.

Psalm 81 calls Israel to a feast, then gives God's own voice: he freed them from Egypt and told them to have no foreign god, but his people would not listen, so he let them go their stubborn way. Read beside chapter 17, which says twice that Israel did not listen, the psalm sounds like God's side of the same story, though the pairing is an interpretation.
