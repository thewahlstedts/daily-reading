The northern kingdom comes to its end this week. In chapter 15, Azariah of Judah (also called Uzziah) does right, but God strikes him with a skin disease and his son Jotham governs. In Israel, Jeroboam's son Zechariah is killed after six months, which the text notes fulfills God's word that Jehu's line would rule to the fourth generation. Shallum lasts a month; Menahem pays the king of Assyria a thousand talents of silver to keep his hold on the kingdom; Pekahiah is killed by his officer Pekah; and in Pekah's days Tiglath-Pileser of Assyria takes Galilee, Gilead and all Naphtali and deports the people. In chapter 16, Ahaz of Judah even makes his son pass through the fire. When Aram and Israel attack Jerusalem, he calls himself the servant and son of the king of Assyria and pays him with silver and gold from the temple. Then, after seeing an altar in Damascus, he has the priest Uriah build a copy, makes it the main altar, and moves God's bronze altar aside.

Chapter 17 tells the fall. Hoshea stops paying tribute and sends to Egypt, and the king of Assyria imprisons him, besieges Samaria for three years, captures it, and deports Israel. The text stops to explain why: Israel feared other gods, built high places, made two metal calves, served Baal and passed their children through the fire, though God had warned Israel and Judah through every prophet to turn back. They did not listen, so God removed them from his presence, leaving only Judah, which also failed to keep his commands. The foreigners settled in Samaria's towns fear God while serving their own idols.

Chapters 18 to 20 bring a different kind of king. Hezekiah trusts God as no other king of Judah did, removes the high places, and breaks up the bronze serpent Moses made, because people had been burning incense to it. He rebels against Assyria, and Sennacherib takes Judah's fortified cities. Hezekiah pays him with the temple's silver and even its overlaid doors, yet Assyria's officers still come to Jerusalem and tell the people on the wall that no nation's god has saved it from Assyria. In chapter 19, Hezekiah goes to the temple in sackcloth and sends to the prophet Isaiah, who answers that God will send the king home by a rumor. When a threatening letter comes, Hezekiah spreads it out before God and prays that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that he alone is God. Isaiah brings God's answer: he will turn Sennacherib back the way he came and defend the city for his own sake and David's. That night an angel of God strikes the Assyrian camp, and Sennacherib returns to Nineveh, where he is killed while worshiping his god. In chapter 20, God sees Hezekiah's tears in sickness and adds fifteen years to his life, with the shadow going back ten steps as a sign. But Hezekiah shows envoys from Babylon all his treasures, and Isaiah says that everything in the palace, and some of his own offspring, will one day be carried to Babylon.

Psalm 81 calls Israel to a feast, then gives God's own voice: he freed them from Egypt and told them to have no foreign god, but his people would not listen, so he let them go their stubborn way. Beside chapter 17, which says twice that Israel did not listen, the psalm sounds like God's side of the same story, though the pairing is an interpretation.
