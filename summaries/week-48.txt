The Song of Songs, "which is for Solomon," is a set of love poems in which a woman and her beloved speak to each other, with the young women of Jerusalem also addressed. In chapters 1–2 she longs for his kisses, says she is dark from the sun after her mother's sons set her to keep the vineyards, and asks where he pastures his flock. They praise each other in images of perfume, doves, a lily and an apple tree. She asks the young women not to awaken love until it pleases, a refrain that returns twice more. Then he comes bounding over the hills like a gazelle and calls her out because winter is past and the flowers have appeared, and she says that her beloved is hers and she is his.

In chapters 3–4 she searches the city at night for the one she loves, finds him and will not let him go. Then Solomon's carriage comes up from the desert, guarded by sixty warriors, on his wedding day. He then praises his bride's beauty, calls her a locked garden and a sealed spring, and she invites him into his garden. In chapters 5–6 she is slow to open the door when he knocks at night, and when she does, he is gone; the watchmen beat her as she searches. Asked what makes her beloved better than others, she describes him from head to foot. He calls her his one perfect dove, and others call to the Shulammite to turn so they may look at her. Chapters 7–8 bring a last song of praise and her invitation to go out to the vineyards together, then the lines: set me as a seal upon your heart, for love is strong as death, and many waters cannot quench it. Readers have taken the Song in different ways, as a celebration of love between a man and a woman, as a picture of God's love for his people, or both; the poems themselves never name God.

Malachi is a series of disputes in which God makes a claim, the people ask "How?", and he answers. In chapter 1 the people ask how God has loved them, and he points to his love for Jacob over Esau, whose land of Edom he has laid waste. Then he accuses the priests of despising his name by offering blind, lame and sick animals they would not offer their governor, while his name is great among the nations from the rising of the sun to its setting. Chapter 2 recalls God's covenant with Levi and charges the priests with causing many to stumble. It then turns to Judah's faithlessness: marrying the daughter of a foreign god, and breaking faith with the wife of one's youth, with God as witness. In the LEB's wording God says he hates divorce, and the people have wearied him by asking where the God of justice is.

In chapter 3 God will send his messenger to prepare the way, and the Lord they seek will come suddenly to his temple, like a refiner's fire, to purify the sons of Levi and judge those who oppress the worker, the widow, the orphan and the foreigner. God has not changed, and he calls them to return to him so that he will return to them. They have robbed him in tithes, and he invites them to bring the whole tithe and test whether he will open the windows of heaven. When some say serving God is useless, those who fear him speak together, and a scroll of remembrance is written for them. In Matthew 11:10 Jesus applies the words about the messenger to John the Baptist.
