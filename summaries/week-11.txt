The house of Ahab falls this week. In chapter 9, a young prophet sent by Elisha anoints the commander Jehu king at Ramoth-Gilead, with orders to destroy Ahab's house and avenge the prophets' blood on Jezebel. At Jezreel, when the wounded Joram asks whether it is peace, Jehu answers that there is no peace amid Jezebel's prostitutions and sorceries, shoots him, and has his body thrown onto Naboth's field, as God had said. Ahaziah of Judah is shot too and dies at Megiddo. Jezebel paints her eyes and taunts Jehu from a window; eunuchs throw her down, and when they go to bury her only her skull, feet and palms are left, as Elijah had said. In chapter 10, at Jehu's word, the leaders of Samaria behead Ahab's seventy sons. Jehu kills the rest of Ahab's house and forty-two of Ahaziah's relatives, then gathers every servant of Baal for a great sacrifice, has them all killed, and turns Baal's temple into a latrine. Yet he keeps the golden calves at Bethel and Dan. God promises his sons the throne to the fourth generation, but Hazael begins taking Israel's land east of the Jordan.

Chapter 11 turns to Judah. Ahaziah's mother Athaliah sets out to destroy the royal family, but his sister Jehosheba hides his son Jehoash and his nurse, and the boy stays hidden in the temple for six years. In the seventh, Jehoiada the priest arms the guards with King David's spears and shields and crowns the boy. Athaliah cries treason and is killed, Jehoiada makes a covenant that the people will be God's people, and the temple of Baal is torn down. In chapter 12, Jehoash does right all his days because Jehoiada instructed him. When the priests still have not repaired the temple by his twenty-third year, Jehoiada sets a chest with a hole in its lid beside the altar, and the money goes to honest workers. Later Jehoash sends the temple's gold to Hazael to turn him from Jerusalem, and his own servants kill him.

In chapter 13, Jehu's son Jehoahaz does evil and Aram oppresses Israel, yet when he pleads, God sends a deliverer. His son Jehoash weeps over the dying Elisha. Elisha has him shoot an arrow of victory toward the east, then strike the ground with arrows; he strikes three times and stops, and Elisha, angry, says he will now defeat Aram only three times. After Elisha's death, a dead man thrown into his grave touches his bones and stands up. In chapter 14, Amaziah of Judah executes his father's killers but spares their sons, as the law of Moses commands (Deuteronomy 24:16). After beating Edom he challenges Jehoash of Israel, who answers with a fable of a thornbush and a cedar, defeats him, breaks down part of Jerusalem's wall and carries off the temple's treasures. In Israel, Jeroboam son of Jehoash does evil, yet restores Israel's borders as the prophet Jonah had said, because God saw Israel's bitter misery and had not decreed to blot out its name.

Psalm 80 calls on the shepherd of Israel to stir up his power before Ephraim, Benjamin and Manasseh, with the refrain that God would restore his people and make his face shine so that they may be saved. It pictures Israel as a vine brought from Egypt that filled the land, and asks why its walls are broken down. Beside a week in which Aram strips Israel's land and God keeps sparing it, the psalm can be heard as the northern kingdom's own prayer, though that link is an interpretation.
