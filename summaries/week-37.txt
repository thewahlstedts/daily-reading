Chapter 14 ends Job's long reply from the week before. A human being born of a woman, he says, is short of days and full of trouble, like a flower that withers or a passing shadow. There is hope for a tree: cut it down and at the scent of water it sprouts again. But a man lies down and does not rise. Job wishes God would hide him in Sheol until his anger passes and then remember him, and he asks whether a man who dies will live again. As it is, he says, God destroys human hope the way water wears away stone.

The second round of speeches begins in chapter 15. Eliphaz tells Job that his words are wind, that he is doing away with the fear of God, and that his own mouth condemns him. He asks whether Job was born before the hills or has listened in on God's council, and points out that the gray-haired are on the friends' side. He repeats that no human can be clean before God, and describes the wicked man who lives in terror and does not stay rich. In chapter 16 Job calls them all miserable comforters and says that in their place he could talk the same way. He describes God as an enemy who has torn him, seized him by the neck and set him up as a target, though there is no violence on his hands and his prayer is pure. Yet he also says that his witness is in heaven and the one who vouches for him is on high. Chapter 17 sinks again: his spirit is broken, he has become a byword, and if his only home is Sheol, where is his hope.

In chapter 18 Bildad the Shuhite asks how long Job will go on, and why the friends are treated as stupid. He paints the fate of the wicked: his lamp goes out, traps catch his heel, terrors chase him, disease eats his skin, he is torn from his tent and brought to the king of terrors, and his name is forgotten. This, he ends, is the place of one who does not know God; a reader may notice how closely the picture matches Job's own losses. In chapter 19 Job asks how long they will crush him with words. He says plainly that God has wronged him and walled up his way, and lists those who have turned away: kin, close friends, servants, even his wife and the little boys. He begs his friends to pity him, for the hand of God has touched him. Then he declares that he knows that his redeemer lives and at the last will stand upon the earth, and that he himself will see God with his own eyes. Readers and traditions understand this redeemer in different ways; the text does not name him.

Psalms 112 and 113 close the week. Psalm 112 describes the person who fears the LORD and delights in his commands: generous, unafraid of bad news, his heart secure in the LORD, his righteousness enduring forever. Psalm 113 calls on all to bless the LORD's name from sunrise to sunset; he is enthroned on high yet stoops to look, raises the poor from the dust and lifts the needy from the ash heap to sit with princes, and gives the childless woman a home as a happy mother. One way to read the pairing is that the God of Psalm 113 is the one Job, sitting among the ashes (2:8), still hopes to see.
