In chapter 38 the LORD answers Job from the storm. He asks who is darkening counsel with words without knowledge, and tells Job to brace himself like a man and answer. God asks where Job was when he laid the earth's foundation while the morning stars sang together, and who shut in the sea and told it how far to come. He asks whether Job has commanded the morning or had the gates of death revealed to him, and whether he can bind the Pleiades, loosen Orion, send out the lightning, or hunt prey for the lion. Chapter 39 moves through the animal world: mountain goats giving birth, the wild donkey that scorns the city, the wild ox that will not plow, the ostrich that leaves its eggs on the ground yet laughs at horse and rider, the war horse that laughs at danger, and the hawk and eagle. In all this God does not explain Job's suffering or mention the scene before the LORD in chapters 1 and 2.

In chapter 40 God asks whether a faultfinder will contend with Shaddai. Job answers that he is small and puts his hand over his mouth; he has spoken once, even twice, and will say no more. God speaks again from the storm and asks whether Job would annul his justice and condemn him to make himself right. If Job can humble the proud with an arm like God's, God will grant that his own right hand can save him. Then God describes Behemoth, which he made just as he made Job, and in chapter 41 Leviathan, which no hook or harpoon can take, king over all the proud. In chapter 42 Job says he knows God can do all things; he spoke of things too wonderful for him that he did not understand. He had heard of God by the hearing of the ear, but now his eye has seen him, and he repents in dust and ashes. Then the LORD tells Eliphaz that his anger burns against him and his two friends, because they have not spoken what is right as his servant Job has. They are to offer burnt offerings, and Job prays for them. The LORD restores Job's fortunes, giving him twice what he had, and his family come to comfort him. He has seven sons and three daughters again, named Jemimah, Keziah and Qeren-Happuk, who receive an inheritance with their brothers, and he lives another 140 years.

Psalm 119 is by far the longest psalm, 176 verses in 22 sections of eight, each headed by a letter of the Hebrew alphabet in order. Nearly every verse names God's word under some term: law, testimonies, precepts, statutes, commands, ordinances, word. The same notes return throughout: delight in God's law, prayers to be taught and given understanding, requests to be revived according to his word, and loyalty kept while the arrogant mock and persecute. Lines include hiding God's word in the heart (verse 11), affliction that taught the psalmist God's statutes (verses 67 and 71), and God's word as a lamp to the feet and a light to the path (verse 105). It ends not with triumph but with the admission that he has wandered like a lost sheep and the plea for God to seek his servant.

Psalms 120 and 121 begin the songs of ascents. In Psalm 120 the psalmist, living among people who hate peace, asks to be rescued from lying lips. Psalm 121 lifts its eyes to the mountains and finds help in the LORD, maker of heaven and earth, who does not slumber or sleep and will guard going out and coming in. Read after Job, one way to hear this psalm is beside Job's complaint about God as a watcher who made him a target (7:20): here the one who watches is a protector.
