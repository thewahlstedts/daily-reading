Chapter 7 opens with a run of sayings about what is better: a good name than precious ointment, the house of mourning than the house of feasting, the rebuke of the wise than the song of fools, patience than a hot temper. In prosperity rejoice and in adversity consider, for God has made the one as well as the other. The Teacher has seen the righteous perish and the wicked live long; he warns against being excessively righteous or excessively wicked, and admits that there is no one on earth who does good and never sins. All he found is that God made humans upright, but they have devised many schemes. Chapter 8 counsels keeping the king's command and notes that no one controls the day of his death. He says it will go well with those who fear God, though he admits the righteous sometimes get what the wicked deserve. So he commends enjoyment, and concludes that no one, however wise, can find out the work of God.

Chapter 9 says the righteous and the wise are in God's hand, yet the same fate comes to all, good and wicked alike. A living dog is better than a dead lion, for the dead know nothing. So eat your food with joy, enjoy life with the wife you love, and do whatever your hand finds to do with all your might, because in Sheol no one works or plans. The race is not to the swift nor the battle to the strong, for time and chance happen to them all. A poor wise man once saved a small city from a great king, yet the wisdom of the poor is despised; wisdom is better than weapons, but one sinner destroys much good. Chapter 10 collects sayings about folly and rulers: dead flies spoil the perfumer's ointment, a fool's own lips devour him, and through laziness the roof sinks in.

Chapter 11 urges sending your bread out on the water and sowing in the morning and evening, because no one knows which will prosper or how God does his work. Light is sweet, so let the young rejoice in their youth, knowing that God will bring them into judgment for all of it. Chapter 12 tells the reader to remember the Creator in the days of youth, before the days of trouble, in a poem of things winding down, often read as a picture of old age: the guards tremble, the grinders cease, those at the windows see dimly, the silver cord snaps, and the dust returns to the earth and the breath to God who gave it. After the Teacher's refrain sounds once more, that everything is vanity, an epilogue praises the Teacher's care with words and ends with the conclusion: fear God and keep his commandments, for God will bring every deed into judgment, including every secret thing.

Psalms 125–127 are songs of ascents. In Psalm 125 those who trust the LORD are like Mount Zion, which cannot be moved, and as the mountains surround Jerusalem, so the LORD surrounds his people. Psalm 126 remembers when God restored Zion's fortunes and mouths filled with laughter, and asks him to do it again: those who sow in tears will reap with joy. Psalm 127, titled "Of Solomon," says that unless the LORD builds the house its builders labor in vain, and that children are a heritage from him. The plan sets it right after Ecclesiastes, and one way to read that pairing is that it answers the Teacher's question about what toil gains: what the LORD builds is not in vain.
