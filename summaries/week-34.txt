In Esther 3 King Ahasuerus promotes Haman the Agagite above all his officials. Everyone at the king's gate bows to him except Mordecai, who has told them he is a Jew. Enraged, Haman decides to destroy not just Mordecai but all the Jews in the kingdom. Lots, called pur, are cast to choose a day, and it falls in the twelfth month, Adar. Haman tells the king that a scattered people do not keep the king's laws, the king hands him his signet ring, and letters go out ordering every Jew, young and old, to be killed on the thirteenth of Adar. The king and Haman sit down to drink while Susa is bewildered.

In chapter 4 Mordecai puts on sackcloth and cries aloud in the city, and the Jews in every province mourn and fast. He urges Esther to plead with the king. She replies that anyone who goes to the king uninvited is put to death unless he holds out his gold scepter. Mordecai answers that if she keeps silent, relief will come for the Jews from somewhere else, and perhaps she has come to her royal position for such a time as this. Esther asks all the Jews in Susa to fast for her for three days and says she will go, and if she perishes, she perishes. In chapter 5 the king holds out the scepter and offers her up to half the kingdom, but she only invites him and Haman to a banquet, then to a second one the next day. Furious that Mordecai still will not rise for him, Haman, on his wife Zeresh's advice, builds a gallows fifty cubits high for Mordecai.

Chapter 6 turns on a sleepless night. The king has the royal records read and finds that Mordecai once exposed a plot against his life and was never rewarded. Haman, arriving to ask for Mordecai's hanging, is asked how to honor a man the king wishes to honor; sure it means himself, he describes a royal robe, horse and proclamation, and then must do it all for Mordecai. In chapter 7, at the second banquet, Esther asks for her life and her people's and names Haman as the enemy, and Haman is hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai. In chapter 8 the king gives Haman's house to Esther and his signet ring to Mordecai, and since a sealed royal decree cannot be revoked, a new one lets the Jews defend themselves on the same day.

In chapter 9, on the thirteenth of Adar, the day their enemies hoped to overpower them, the Jews defeat those who attack them, and the text says three times that they did not touch the plunder. Afterward Mordecai and Esther write to establish the yearly feast of Purim, named after the pur, as days of feasting and of gifts to one another and to the poor, because the month turned from sorrow to joy. Chapter 10 ends with Mordecai second only to the king, seeking the good of his people. God is never named in the book. One way to read Haman's title, the Agagite, is as an echo of Agag king of Amalek, whom Saul spared along with the best of the plunder (1 Samuel 15:8-9), set against Mordecai, a Benjaminite descended from Kish (Esther 2:5), the name of Saul's father (1 Samuel 9:1-2); the text itself does not draw that line.
