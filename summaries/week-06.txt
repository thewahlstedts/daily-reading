1 Kings opens with David old and unable to keep warm, cared for by the young Abishag. In chapter 1, his son Adonijah, whom David had never rebuked, declares himself king and holds a sacrificial feast with Joab and Abiathar the priest, leaving out Nathan, Zadok, Benaiah and Solomon. Bathsheba and then Nathan go in to David and remind him of his oath that Solomon would reign after him. David keeps it that same day: Zadok and Nathan anoint Solomon at Gihon, and the people shout, "Long live King Solomon," so loudly that the earth shakes. Adonijah's guests scatter, and Adonijah clings to the horns of the altar until Solomon promises he will live if he proves worthy.

In chapter 2, David charges Solomon to be strong and keep God's commands written in the law of Moses, so that God will keep his promise that David's line will not be cut off from the throne. He also tells Solomon to deal with Joab, who killed Abner and Amasa in peacetime, to be loyal to Barzillai's sons, and not to let Shimei go unpunished. Then David dies. When Adonijah asks, through Bathsheba, to marry Abishag, Solomon treats it as a bid for the kingdom and has Benaiah kill him. Abiathar is banished to Anathoth, fulfilling, the text says, God's word about the house of Eli. Joab flees to the altar and is killed there, and Shimei, who had sworn not to leave Jerusalem, is put to death after going to Gath to fetch runaway slaves. The chapter ends with the kingdom established in Solomon's hand.

Chapter 3 says Solomon loved God but sacrificed at the high places, since no temple had yet been built. At Gibeon God appears to him in a dream and tells him to ask for anything. Solomon asks for a listening heart to judge God's people and tell good from bad. God is pleased that he did not ask for long life, riches or his enemies' death, and gives him wisdom, along with riches and honor, and long life if he walks in God's ways. Two women then come before him, each claiming the same living baby; Solomon calls for a sword and orders the child divided, and gives the baby to the woman who would rather lose him than see him killed. All Israel sees that the wisdom of God is in him. Chapter 4 lists his officials and twelve district governors, describes Judah and Israel living securely, each under their own vine and fig tree, and tells of his proverbs and songs and of people from all nations coming to hear his wisdom.

In chapter 5, Solomon tells Hiram king of Tyre that God has given him rest and that he will build the house for God's name promised to David's son. Hiram supplies cedar and cypress, and Solomon conscripts forced labor from all Israel. Chapter 6 describes the temple, built of stones finished at the quarry, lined with cedar, overlaid with gold, with two olive-wood cherubim in the inner sanctuary. In the middle of the building, God tells Solomon that if he keeps his commands, God will fulfill his promise to David and dwell among Israel. The house is finished in seven years. Psalm 74, a maskil of Asaph, mourns a sanctuary burned and smashed by enemies, then recalls God as king from long ago who split the sea; the plan sets this lament right after the temple is built, a contrast the text itself does not draw.
