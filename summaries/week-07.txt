The week opens with Solomon's building. Chapter 7 records his own palace, which took thirteen years (the temple took seven, 6:38), including the House of the Forest of Lebanon and a hall where he sat to judge. Then Hiram, a bronze worker from Tyre whose mother was a widow from Naphtali, casts the two bronze pillars named Jakin and Boaz, the great basin called the sea standing on twelve bronze oxen, and ten bronze stands with basins, while Solomon has the gold furnishings made for inside. Chapter 8 is the dedication. The priests carry the ark, which holds only the two stone tablets from Horeb, into the most holy place, and the cloud of God's glory fills the house so that the priests cannot stay to serve. Solomon admits that even the highest heavens cannot contain God, then asks him to hear from heaven whatever is prayed toward this house: by Israelites who sin and turn back, by foreigners who come because of his name, and by captives in a distant land. The chapter ends with huge sacrifices and a fourteen-day festival.

Then the tone shifts. In chapter 9, God appears to Solomon a second time with a conditional promise: if he walks as David did, his throne will stand, but if he or his descendants turn to other gods, Israel will be cut off from the land and become a byword among the peoples, and the temple will become a heap of ruins. The same chapter has a sour note: Hiram king of Tyre (not the bronze worker) is unhappy with the twenty towns in Galilee that Solomon gives him. Chapter 10 shows the wealth at its height. The queen of Sheba is left breathless, and Solomon takes in 666 talents of gold in a year, makes an ivory throne and gold shields, and imports horses and chariots from Egypt. Those are the things Deuteronomy 17:16-17 tells a king not to multiply: horses, especially by sending people back to Egypt for them, wives, and silver and gold. Chapter 11 supplies the wives. They turn his heart, he builds high places for Chemosh and Molech, and God says he will tear the kingdom away, though not in Solomon's lifetime, leaving one tribe to his son for David's sake. God raises up adversaries, Hadad of Edom and Rezon, and the prophet Ahijah tears a new cloak into twelve pieces and gives Jeroboam ten. Solomon dies, and his son Rehoboam succeeds him.

Chapter 12 brings the fallout. Rehoboam rejects the elders' advice for the harsher advice of the young men he grew up with, the northern tribes break away from David's house, and they make Jeroboam king of Israel; only Judah stays with Rehoboam. Afraid that worship in Jerusalem will draw the people back, Jeroboam sets up golden calves at Bethel and Dan. What God announced in chapter 11 has come to pass; the heavier warning of chapter 9, exile and a ruined temple, still lies ahead. Psalms 75–76 close the week with God as the judge who brings one down and lifts up another, who broke the bow, the shield and the sword, and who is known in Judah, with his dwelling in Salem and Zion. Read right after the kingdom splits, they make a fitting counterpoint, though that pairing is an interpretation, not something the text states.
