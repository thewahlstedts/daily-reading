Chapter 24 shows Joash doing right all the days of Jehoiada the priest. The people gladly bring the tax Moses levied, and the house of God is restored. But after Jehoiada dies the princes win Joash over, and Judah turns to Asherah poles and idols. When Jehoiada's son Zechariah, clothed by the Spirit of God, warns that because they have forsaken the LORD he will forsake them, the people stone him in the temple court at the king's command. The dying Zechariah asks God to see and avenge. One way to read Luke 11:51 (week 20), where Jesus mentions a Zechariah who perished between the altar and the temple, is as a reference to this man. A small Aramean army defeats Judah's large one, and Joash's own servants kill him in his bed.

In chapter 25 Amaziah does right, though not wholeheartedly. He executes his father's killers but spares their children, as the law of Moses commands (Deuteronomy 24:16). At a prophet's word he sends home the soldiers he hired from Israel and defeats Edom. Then he brings home the gods of Edom and worships them, silences the prophet who rebukes him, and picks a fight with Joash king of Israel. Judah is routed, Jerusalem's wall is broken, and Amaziah is later killed in a conspiracy. In chapters 26–27 Uzziah seeks God, and as long as he does, God makes him prosper, and his fame reaches Egypt. But when he is strong his heart grows proud, and he enters the temple to burn incense, which belongs to the priests. As he rages at the priests who confront him, leprosy breaks out on his forehead, and he lives the rest of his life in a separate house, cut off from the house of the LORD. His son Jotham grows strong because he orders his ways before God.

Chapter 28 is the low point. Ahaz makes idols for the Baals and burns his sons in the fire. Aram and Israel both defeat him, and Israel carries off a great many people of Judah as captives, until the prophet Oded and some leaders of Ephraim send them home clothed, fed and anointed, as far as Jericho. Ahaz turns to the king of Assyria, who brings him trouble instead of help, then sacrifices to the gods of Damascus, cuts up the temple's vessels and shuts its doors. In chapter 29 his son Hezekiah opens those doors in the first month of his first year. The Levites cleanse the house, the priests make atonement for all Israel, and worship resumes with David's instruments and songs, to the joy of the people. In chapter 30 Hezekiah invites all Israel and Judah to keep the Passover, urging them to return to a gracious and compassionate God. Many in the north mock the invitation, but some come. When many eat without being cleansed, Hezekiah prays that God will pardon everyone who sets his heart to seek him, and God heals the people. The feast is extended by seven days, and there has been no joy like it in Jerusalem since Solomon.

Psalm 97 proclaims that the LORD is king, with righteousness and justice the foundation of his throne. Those who serve images are put to shame, Zion is glad, and those who love God are told to hate evil. After a week of idols set up and then torn down, the psalm can be read as a fitting close, though that pairing is an interpretation.
