The genealogies finish this week, and the story of David begins. Chapter 8 traces Benjamin's family and ends with Saul's line: his father Kish, his sons, among them Jonathan and Eshbaal, and Jonathan's descendants down several generations. Chapter 9 sums up: all Israel was recorded in the genealogies, and Judah was taken to Babylon because of its sin. Then it lists those who settled in Jerusalem, people from Judah, Benjamin, Ephraim and Manasseh, along with priests and Levites. It gives special attention to the gatekeepers, whom David and Samuel had appointed for their faithfulness and who opened the house of God each morning, and to those who prepared the bread and sang day and night. The chapter closes by repeating Saul's family line.

Chapter 10 tells only Saul's last day. The Philistines rout Israel on Mount Gilboa and kill Jonathan and two of his brothers; Saul, wounded by archers, falls on his own sword when his armor-bearer will not kill him. The Philistines fasten his head in the temple of Dagon, and the men of Jabesh-Gilead recover the bodies and bury them. The text then gives its judgment: Saul died for his sin, for not keeping God's command and for consulting a medium instead of seeking God, so God turned the kingdom over to David son of Jesse.

In chapter 11, all Israel comes to David at Hebron, reminding him that God had said he would shepherd his people, and the elders anoint him king according to the word given through Samuel. David takes the stronghold of Zion from the Jebusites; Joab goes up first and becomes commander. Then come David's mighty men. One scene stands out: when David longs aloud for water from the well by Bethlehem's gate, three of them break through the Philistine camp to bring it, and David will not drink it but pours it out before God, saying it is the blood of men who risked their lives. Chapter 12 lists those who joined David even before he was king: Benjaminites, Saul's own kinsmen, who came to him at Ziklag and could sling and shoot with either hand, and Gadites with faces like lions who came to his fortress toward the wilderness. When some come to his stronghold, David asks whether they come in peace or to betray him, and the Spirit comes on Amasai, who answers that they are his and wishes peace to him and his helpers, for his God helps him. Then the tribes arrive at Hebron with one heart to make David king, and there are three days of feasting and great joy in Israel.

In chapter 13, David proposes bringing the ark of God back, since they had not sought it in Saul's days, and the whole assembly agrees. They carry it from Kiriath-Jearim on a new cart with music and celebration, but when the oxen stumble and Uzza reaches out to steady it, God strikes him down. David is angry, then afraid, and leaves the ark at the house of Obed-Edom, where for three months God blesses his household. Psalm 86, a prayer of David, asks God to hear the poor and needy, calls him compassionate, gracious, slow to anger and abundant in loyal love, and asks him to unite the heart to fear his name. One way to read the pairing is that after a week of fear and joy around God's presence, it offers words for coming near to him.
