In chapter 18, David stays in the city at his troops' urging and orders his three commanders, Joab, Abishai and Ittai, to deal gently with the young man Absalom. Israel is defeated in the forest of Ephraim, where the forest takes more lives than the sword. Absalom's head catches in a great oak as his mule runs on, and though one soldier refuses to touch him because of the king's order, Joab drives three spears into him and his armor bearers finish him off. When two runners bring the news, David asks each whether the young man is safe, and when the Cushite makes it plain that Absalom is dead, David goes up to the room over the gate weeping, "My son, Absalom, my son," wishing he had died instead.

In chapter 19, the victory turns to mourning until Joab bluntly warns David that he is shaming the men who saved his life, and the king goes and sits in the gate. David wins back Judah and names Amasa, Absalom's commander, to replace Joab. At the Jordan, Shimei begs forgiveness and David swears he will not die. Mephibosheth, unwashed since the day David fled, says Ziba deceived him, and David divides the land between them. The chapter ends with Israel and Judah quarrelling over who has the greater claim on the king. In chapter 20, Sheba son of Bicri calls Israel away from David, and the men of Israel follow him. Joab kills Amasa with a sword while greeting him as a brother, and besieges Sheba in Abel Beth Maacah until a wise woman of the city persuades its people to throw Sheba's head over the wall.

In chapter 21, a three-year famine comes, and God tells David it is because of Saul's bloodguilt in killing the Gibeonites, whom Israel had sworn to spare. David hands seven of Saul's descendants to the Gibeonites but spares Mephibosheth because of his oath to Jonathan. Rizpah, Saul's concubine and mother of two of those executed, guards their bodies from birds and animals until rain falls. David then has the bones of Saul and Jonathan buried in the tomb of Kish, and God answers prayer for the land. Short notes follow of David's men killing Philistine giants, one of them after David grows weary in battle. Chapter 22 is David's song of deliverance, nearly the same as Psalm 18: God is his rock and fortress, who heard him from his temple, came down in storm and fire, and drew him out of deep waters. Chapter 23 gives David's last words, about a ruler who rules righteously in the fear of God and the everlasting covenant God made with his house, then lists his mighty men. Three of them break through the Philistine lines to bring him water from the well of Bethlehem, and he pours it out to God rather than drink what cost them such risk. The list ends with Uriah the Hittite.

In chapter 24, God is angry with Israel and incites David to count the people. Joab objects but obeys, and afterward David's heart strikes him and he confesses his sin. The prophet Gad offers three punishments; David chooses to fall into God's hands, because his compassion is great, and a plague kills many. When the angel reaches out toward Jerusalem, God relents, and David asks that the punishment fall on him and his family instead of the people. At Gad's word he buys Araunah's threshing floor, refusing to offer God sacrifices that cost him nothing, builds an altar there, and the plague stops.
