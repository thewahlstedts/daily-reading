In chapter 9 Israel is told not to rejoice, because it has played the whore and left its God; Ephraim will go back to Egypt and eat unclean food in Assyria. God found Israel like grapes in the wilderness, but now they will be wanderers among the nations. Chapter 10 calls Israel a luxuriant vine that built more altars the more it prospered. The calf of Beth-aven will be carried off to Assyria, and people will tell the mountains to cover them. In the middle comes a call: sow righteousness, reap loyal love, break up your fallow ground, for it is time to seek the LORD.

Chapter 11 is God speaking as a parent: when Israel was a child he loved him and called his son out of Egypt, and taught Ephraim to walk, but when he called, they went away and sacrificed to the Baals. Yet God cries out, How can I give you up, Ephraim? His heart is disturbed and his compassion stirred; he will not destroy Ephraim again, for he is God and not a mortal, the Holy One among them. Matthew 2:15 applies the words "out of Egypt I called my son" to Jesus. Chapter 12 recalls Jacob, who deceived his brother in the womb and struggled with the angel, and calls his descendants to return to God, keep love and justice, and wait for him. In chapter 13 the people who were fed in the desert grew full, proud and forgetful, and God will meet them like a lion or a bear robbed of her cubs. The book ends in chapter 14 with an invitation to return, taking words of confession: Assyria will not save us, and we will no longer call the work of our hands our god. God says he will heal their faithlessness and love them freely, and be like dew to Israel, which will blossom like a lily. The ways of the LORD are right, the last verse says, and the righteous walk in them.

In Haggai 1, dated to the second year of King Darius, the people say it is not yet time to rebuild the LORD's house, while they live in paneled houses. God tells them to consider their ways: they sow much and harvest little because his house lies in ruins. Zerubbabel the governor, Joshua the high priest and the remnant of the people listen, God says "I am with you," and within the month the work begins. In chapter 2 God asks who remembers the house in its former glory and whether this one seems like nothing, then tells them to take courage and work, for his spirit is with them; he will shake the nations, and the latter glory of this house will be greater than the former. After a ruling from the priests shows that the people's offerings have been unclean, God promises to bless them from the day the foundation is laid, and he promises to make Zerubbabel like a signet ring, for he has chosen him.

Psalms 128–130 are songs of ascents. Psalm 128 blesses the one who fears the LORD with children like olive shoots around the table. In Psalm 129 Israel says it has been attacked since its youth, yet no one has prevailed against it. Psalm 130 calls to God out of the depths: if he kept a record of sins, no one could stand, but with him there is forgiveness, so the soul waits for the Lord more than watchmen wait for the morning. Coming after Hosea's call to return, its closing promise that God will redeem Israel from all its iniquities reads, in one view, like an answer to that call.
