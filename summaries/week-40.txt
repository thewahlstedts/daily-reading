With Job's words ended and the three friends silent, a new speaker appears in chapter 32. Elihu son of Barakel the Buzite, younger than the others, has held back because they are older. Now he is angry: at Job for justifying himself rather than God, and at the three friends for finding no answer yet declaring Job guilty. He says that wisdom comes not with years but from the breath of Shaddai, and that he is full of words like new wineskins ready to burst. He speaks without interruption through chapter 37, no one answers him, and the book does not say whether God approves of his words.

In chapter 33 Elihu addresses Job by name and says he too was formed from clay, so Job need not fear him. He quotes Job's claim to be clean and to be treated as God's enemy, and replies that God is greater than any human and speaks in more than one way: in dreams and night visions to turn people from pride, and through pain on a sickbed. If a messenger, a mediator, stands by such a person, the sufferer may be spared from the pit because a ransom has been found, and the sufferer will be restored, pray and see God's face with joy. In chapter 34 he turns to the wise listeners. Job, he says, claims to be righteous and says God has taken away his justice, and has said it does no good to delight in God. Far be it from God to do wrong, Elihu answers; he repays people for their deeds, shows no favor to princes, and sees every step. He concludes that Job speaks without knowledge. Chapter 35 argues that human sin or righteousness neither harms nor helps God; people cry out under oppression but do not seek God their Maker, who gives songs in the night.

In chapter 36 Elihu says he still has more to say on God's behalf. God is mighty but despises no one; he opens people's ears through affliction, and those who listen live in prosperity while those who will not perish. He warns Job to take care not to turn to evil. Then he praises God's greatness in the rain, the clouds and the lightning, and in chapter 37 the thunder of God's voice, the snow, the ice and the storm. He tells Job to stand still and consider God's wonders, asks whether Job can spread out the skies with him, and ends that God is beyond reach, great in power and justice, and not impressed by those who think themselves wise. One way to read this closing description of the storm is as a lead into what happens next, when God answers Job from the storm (38:1).

Psalm 118 closes the week with thanksgiving. It opens and ends with the call to give thanks to the LORD, for he is good and his loyal love is forever, and Israel, the house of Aaron and all who fear the LORD repeat that refrain. The psalmist called in distress and the LORD answered; with the LORD on his side he does not fear what people can do. Nations surrounded him like bees, but in the LORD's name he stood against them. The LORD disciplined him severely but did not give him over to death. He asks for the gates of righteousness to be opened, says that the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone, and rejoices in the day the LORD has acted. Peter applies that stone saying to Jesus in Acts 4:11, which the plan reaches in two weeks.
