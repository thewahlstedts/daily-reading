In chapter 26 Job answers Bildad with irony, asking how much help he has really given to one without strength or wisdom. Then he speaks of God's power: Sheol lies naked before him, he hangs the earth over nothing, stilled the sea and struck down Rahab. These, Job says, are only the fringes of his ways, a faint whisper; who could understand the thunder of his power. In chapter 27 he swears by God, who he says has taken away his justice, that as long as he has breath he will not speak falsely or admit that his friends are right. The rest of the chapter describes the portion of the wicked: children born for the sword, silver heaped up that the righteous will wear and the innocent divide, and a storm that carries him away in the night.

Chapter 28 is a poem on wisdom, set within Job's words. People mine silver, gold and iron, cut shafts far from where anyone lives, and bring hidden things to light by paths no bird of prey has seen. But where can wisdom be found? It is not in the land of the living; the deep and the sea say it is not in them, and it cannot be bought with the gold of Ophir or any jewel. Only God understands its way and knows its place, and to human beings he has said that the fear of the Lord is wisdom and turning from evil is understanding. Much the same words, fearing God and turning from evil, describe Job himself in 1:1.

Chapters 29 to 31 are Job's closing statement. In chapter 29 he remembers the days when God watched over him and his children were around him. When he went to the city gate, young men stepped aside, the old stood, and officials fell silent. He rescued the poor and the orphan, made the widow's heart sing, was eyes to the blind and feet to the lame, and a father to the poor; it reads as an answer to the charges Eliphaz made in chapter 22. In chapter 30 the picture reverses: men whose fathers he would not have set with his sheepdogs now mock him and spit in his face. He cries to God and gets no answer, and says God has turned cruel to him. He hoped for good, and evil came. In chapter 31 he swears that he has not looked with lust, lied, lain in wait at a neighbor's door, denied his slaves justice, eaten while the orphan went hungry, let the poor go without clothing, trusted in gold, worshiped the sun or moon, or rejoiced at an enemy's ruin, and for several of these he calls penalties down on himself if he is lying. The same God, he says, made his slaves and him in the womb. He signs his defense and asks Shaddai to answer him. The chapter closes with the line that the words of Job are ended.

Psalms 116 and 117 close the week. In Psalm 116 the psalmist loves the LORD because he heard his cry when the ropes of death were around him. He says that in his haste he called everyone a liar, and asks what he can give back for all God's benefits; he will lift the cup of salvation, offer thanksgiving and pay his vows before all the people in Jerusalem. Psalm 117, just two verses, calls all nations to praise the LORD for his loyal love and lasting faithfulness. The plan sets this answered prayer after Job's unanswered one, which one reader might take as contrast and another as a glimpse of where Job's story is heading.
