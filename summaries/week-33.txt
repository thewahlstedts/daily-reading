In Hebrews 10 the law's yearly sacrifices remind people of sins but cannot take them away. Christ came to do God's will, offered one sacrifice for sins for all time and sat down at God's right hand. So the author urges readers to draw near, hold fast to their hope, stir one another to love and good works, and keep meeting together. Then comes the second sharp warning: for those who keep sinning deliberately after knowing the truth, no sacrifice for sins remains, only judgment, and it is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God. As with chapter 6, Christian traditions understand this warning in different ways. The author again turns at once to encouragement, recalling how the readers once endured suffering and the loss of their property with joy.

Chapter 11 defines faith as the assurance of what is hoped for, the proof of things not seen, and then walks through Israel's story by faith: Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham leaving home without knowing where he was going and later offering Isaac, reasoning that God could raise the dead. Then come Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses choosing mistreatment with God's people over the pleasures of Egypt, the Red Sea crossing, Jericho and Rahab. The author runs out of time to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel and the prophets, who through faith won victories, and of others who were tortured, imprisoned and killed. None of them received what was promised, because God had provided something better, so that they would not be made perfect without "us," as the author puts it. Chapter 12 draws the lesson: surrounded by this cloud of witnesses, run the race with endurance, looking to Jesus, who endured the cross for the joy set before him. The readers have not come to the terrifying fire of Mount Sinai but to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, so they should not refuse the one who speaks from heaven. Chapter 13 closes with practical instructions: love one another, show hospitality, remember prisoners, honor marriage, avoid the love of money, and offer God a sacrifice of praise. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today and forever. It ends with a blessing and news that Timothy has been released.

Esther begins in the Persian court of King Ahasuerus, who rules 127 provinces from Susa. In chapter 1, at the end of a seven-day feast, merry with wine, he sends for Queen Vashti to show off her beauty. She refuses. Fearing that wives across the empire will follow her example, the king removes her on his advisers' counsel. In chapter 2 young women are gathered from every province for the king. Among them is Esther, an orphan raised by her cousin Mordecai, a Benjaminite whose family was carried into exile from Jerusalem. On Mordecai's instructions she keeps her people secret. She wins favor with everyone, the king loves her more than all the others, and he makes her queen. Mordecai, sitting at the king's gate, learns of a plot against the king and reports it through Esther, and it is written in the royal record. God is not mentioned in these chapters.

Psalm 108 is a song of praise to God, whose loyal love is higher than the heavens, followed by a plea for help against enemies, because human help is worthless.
