The debate goes on. In chapter 8 Bildad the Shuhite asks whether God perverts justice and suggests that if Job's children sinned, God handed them over to their transgression. If Job is pure and seeks God, he says, God will restore him, and his end will be greater than his beginning. He appeals to the wisdom of former generations: papyrus cannot grow without a marsh, and the hope of the godless is as fragile as a spider's house. God will not reject the blameless, he concludes, and will yet fill Job's mouth with laughter. Like Eliphaz, he assumes that suffering points to sin, a view the book does not endorse (42:7).

In chapter 9 Job agrees in part but asks how a human being can be in the right before God. God moves mountains, shakes the earth, made the Bear, Orion and the Pleiades and passes by unseen; no one can answer him once in a thousand. Even if Job is blameless, he says, his own mouth would condemn him, and God destroys the blameless and the wicked alike. He longs for an arbiter who could lay a hand on them both, but there is none. In chapter 10 he speaks to God directly: God's hands shaped him like clay, poured him out like milk and knit him together with bones and sinews, and gave him life and loyal love, yet now God hunts him like a lion. He asks why he was born at all, and begs for a little relief before he goes to the land of darkness.

In chapter 11 Zophar the Naamathite wishes God would speak and show Job the secrets of wisdom, and says God has even overlooked some of Job's guilt. The limits of God are higher than heaven and deeper than Sheol. If Job puts away iniquity and stretches out his hands to God, his life will be brighter than noon and he will remember his misery like water that has flowed past. Job's answer fills chapters 12 and 13. With biting irony he says that wisdom will die with them, notes that he has become a laughingstock though righteous, and points out that the tents of destroyers are at peace. Ask the animals, he says, and they will tell you that the LORD's hand has done this; God humbles counselors, judges, priests and kings. Then he turns on his friends: they whitewash with lies and are worthless healers, and they speak falsely on God's behalf, for which God will rebuke them. He wants to argue his case with God himself. Though God may kill him, he will hope in him and still defend his ways, and he asks God to make known how many his sins are and why God counts him an enemy.

Psalms 110 and 111 close the week. In Psalm 110, a psalm of David, the LORD says to the psalmist's lord to sit at his right hand until his enemies are made his footstool, and swears that he is a priest forever after the manner of Melchizedek, the king of Salem and priest of God Most High who blessed Abram (Genesis 14:18-19). Peter quotes its first verse in Acts 2:34-35. Psalm 111 praises the great works of the LORD, who is gracious and compassionate, feeds those who fear him and remembers his covenant forever, and ends with the fear of the LORD as the beginning of wisdom. Next to the friends' confident wisdom, one way to read that line is as a reminder of where wisdom starts; the pairing is the plan's, not the text's.
